# Break the Spiral, Then Call a Human

The HCI evidence up to October 2026 agrees on a short list of levers for vibe coding. Help people state a checkable intent before the agent writes code. When a session goes wrong, consolidate everything into one spec and restart fresh, rather than piling on corrections. Trust executable evidence over the agent's explanations. Step in rarely, at task boundaries, and with an easy way to dismiss. Keep a human in view, because a human witness changes how people treat AI output. The strongest single result for Codehere is that LLMs get **39% worse on average when a task arrives over several turns**, and putting all the pieces into one prompt recovers **95.1%** of single-turn performance ([Laban et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). A mediator that rewrites vague turns into full instructions recovered **20–24 points** of that loss ([Liu et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.07338.md)). The biggest gap is just as clear. No published framework models a third party that sits between a human and an agent. No shipped tool reads human signals (frustration, repeated restatement) and agent signals (repeated tool calls, the same error) together, across Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and Gemini CLI. I recommend Codehere build an **"unstuck loop"**. It detects a spiral from both sides, pauses at a boundary, and offers a short repair menu. At the centre of that menu is a consolidated restart brief, and the same brief doubles as a redacted "ask a human" help card. Developers still say they would ask a person when they don't trust the AI (**75%**, [Stack Overflow 2025](https://stackoverflow.blog/2025/07/29/developers-remain-willing-but-reluctant-to-use-ai-the-2025-developer-survey-results-are-here)), and experts can answer a well-packed small question in a **median 53 seconds** ([Drosos et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.13621)). Two caveats apply. None of these interventions has been tested end to end with real users of coding agents. And most agent-era evidence is still preprints or vendor posts. So the report ends with a two-week validation plan, not a build plan.

## The evidence base: old principles, new failure data

### Classic principles still set the rules, but they all assume two parties

Amershi et al.'s 18 guidelines (CHI 2019) are still the baseline. The ones that matter most here are G1/G2 (make clear what the system can do, and how well), G3 (time services to context), G9 (efficient correction), G10 (scope services when in doubt), G12 (remember recent interactions) and G18 (notify about changes). But they were "developed and tested on products with graphical user interfaces" such as Word AutoReplace ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction-design)), and Microsoft has not revised them for agents ([MSR publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/)). Horvitz's **mixed-initiative** principles (CHI 1999) give the decision model a mediator needs most. The system estimates the probability that the user has a goal. It acts above one threshold, does nothing below another, and opens a **dialog** in between. Context, such as the user's attention and the cost of a wrong action, moves those thresholds ([Horvitz 1999](https://erichorvitz.com/chi99horvitz.pdf)).

Norman's gulfs of execution and evaluation assume the user already has a clear intention. Subramonyam et al. (CHI 2024) add the **gulf of envisioning**, "the distance between the human's initial intentions and their formulation of a prompt." It has three parts: a capability gap, an instruction gap, and an intentionality gap. The intentionality gap matters most here. Users who state only a goal skip the planning that would give them a benchmark for judging the output ([Subramonyam et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14459v2)). Terry et al. turn this into three alignment tasks: **specification** (what to do), **process** (how) and **evaluation support** (did it work) ([Terry et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00710v2)). These map almost one-to-one onto vague prompts, agents drifting, and over-trust in unchecked output.

Clark & Brennan's **grounding** theory explains why agents build on wrong assumptions. LLM output is **77.5% less likely** than human speech to contain grounding acts such as clarifications and acknowledgements. Preference tuning makes this worse: questions appear in 13.77% of preferred responses but 18.35% of dispreferred ones, so asking gets penalised ([Shaikh et al., NAACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.348)). In WildChat, users clarify about **3×** as often as the model does, and people restart in **10.7%** of sessions rather than repair the conversation ([Shaikh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13975v2)).

On autonomy, Shneiderman rejects the one-dimensional scale. He argues for **high automation and high human control** at once for complex tasks ([Shneiderman 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.04087)). Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000) set automation per stage (information acquisition, analysis, decision, action) and warn of complacency and **skill degradation**. I only had a secondary summary of this paper ([summary](https://andrewclark.super.site/all-media/a-model-for-types-and-levels-of-human-interaction-with-automation)). Feng et al. define agent levels by the user's role: Operator, Collaborator, Consultant, Approver, Observer ([Feng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12469v1)). The trust literature adds **calibration**: trust should match real capability in each situation ([Lee & See 2004](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392)). Automation bias hits experts too, and "is not prevented by training or instructions." It is worst under heavy multitasking, which is exactly what running agents in the background looks like ([Parasuraman & Manzey 2010](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/items/0c787d91-6021-480b-a333-009e0d1d0092/full)).

Two behaviour-change frameworks govern how to step in. **JITAI** design picks decision points and tailoring variables, includes "provide nothing" as a valid option, and delivers only when the person is **receptive** ([Nahum-Shani et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5364076)). Self-determination theory says to protect autonomy, competence and relatedness ([Calvo, Peters & Ryan 2018](https://acola.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/acola-ai-input-paper_human-autonomy-in-ai-systems_calvo-peters-ryan.pdf)). The last of those, relatedness, is the one vibe coding quietly removes.

### Agent-era frameworks name the new gaps

Bansal et al. list what an agent must tell its user: A1 what it can do, A2 what it is about to do, A3 what it is doing, **A4 what side effects it caused**, and **A5 whether the goal was achieved**. They also add X1, how to help the user verify ([Bansal et al. 2024](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2412.10380)). Zhu et al. (2026) keep Amershi's phases and add 14 agent principles. Three matter most here: P10 "Actively Prevent Unhealthy Dependency… protect user competence," P12 "Support Shared Repair," and P13 "tailor recovery to failure type." They argue the 2019 guidelines miss sustained shared control, cross-session memory and recovery from complex failures ([Zhu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20630)). Wang et al. propose four pillars (task alignment, steerability, verifiability, adaptability) and interaction metrics such as **turns to recover intent, intervention frequency and verification effort** ([Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.12355)). OpenAI's governance paper names the open oversight problems: reviewers **rubber-stamping** approvals, stated reasoning that may not match what the model relied on, and traces "too long for users to follow" ([OpenAI 2023](https://cdn.openai.com/papers/practices-for-governing-agentic-ai-systems.pdf)). Magentic-UI shows the main human-in-the-loop mechanisms working together: co-planning, co-tasking, **action guards** on irreversible actions, answer verification, and memory ([Mozannar et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22358v1)).

Read together, these frameworks fit in layers. Horvitz says *when* to step in. Terry, Subramonyam, and Clark & Brennan say *what* to align on. Bansal says *what to communicate*. Shneiderman and Feng say *how much autonomy* to allow. Zhu and Wang add dependency, repair and verifiability. But every one of them assumes a **dyad**, a user and an AI system. None says how a third party should act toward both sides: when to speak to the human, when to instruct the agent, and when to stay quiet. That gap is real. It is also where Codehere can take a position.

### Empirical studies show one consistent vibe-coding failure chain

The **verification tax** was visible from the start. In Copilot sessions, double-checking and editing suggestions took **34.3%** of time ([Mozannar et al., CHI 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.14306)). Users strongly preferred Copilot with no measured speed or success gain, and 7 of 24 threw away faulty code rather than read it ([Vaithilingam et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10366304)). Felt help and real help come apart even more sharply for experts. In METR's RCT, experienced open-source developers were **19% slower** with AI, but believed afterwards that they had been 20% faster ([Becker et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089)).

Vibe coding studies all describe the same loop. Sarkar & Drosos call it **iterative goal satisfaction**. They name **context momentum** (early AI choices lock the project in) and **material disengagement** (people orchestrate rather than write) ([Sarkar & Drosos 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.23253v1)). A grey-literature review coded **skipped QA in 36%** of QA statements, uncritical trust in 18%, and a vulnerable group who "can build software but cannot debug it" ([Fawzy et al., ICSE-SEIP 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.00328v1)). Students in Replit spent 63.6% of their time clicking the prototype and only 7.4% on code. Only **2.2%** of their tests were edge cases, **61%** of prompts asked for debugging, and **4 of 19 restarted the whole project** ([Geng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22614v2)). Professionals behave differently. They plan first, run a mean **2.1 agent steps per prompt**, and rate "one-shotting code without verification" unsuitable 23 to 5 ([Huang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14012v1)). Claude Code telemetry shows the same arc with experience. Auto-approve rises from about 20% to over 40% of sessions, and interrupts *also* rise, from 5% to about 9% of turns ([Anthropic, Feb 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy)). Experts move from **gatekeeping** to **monitoring with targeted interrupts**.

In the wild, human attention is the scarce resource. Of 562 rejected agent PRs, **38% were abandoned or never reviewed** ([Ehsani et al., MSR 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15195v1)). Across 212,297 agent PRs, the median human triage time was 0.2 hours, and **73.9% merged without changes** ([Li, Popescu & Izadi, ICSE 2026 workshop](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2026/jaws-2026-papers/17/Silent-Reliance-or-Diligent-Review-Human-Agent-Engagement-and-Interaction-Patterns-i)). In 20,574 real sessions, the top misalignment symptoms were **constraint violations (38.3%)**, misread intent (27.0%) and **inaccurate self-reporting (22.6%)**. Misalignment **carried over between adjacent sessions** in the same repo ([Tang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29442)).

### Tools and platforms: crowded on the agent side, thin on the human side

Platforms are investing heavily in the agent-side safety boundary. Claude Code's auto mode uses a classifier because users approve **93% of permission prompts**, but it still misses **17% of real "overeager" actions** ([Anthropic Engineering](https://anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode)). Codex swaps the human approval for a reviewer agent, which its own docs call "not a deterministic guarantee" ([Codex docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/codex/sandboxing/auto-review)). The key technical fact for Codehere is that every major CLI now exposes hooks. Claude Code's `UserPromptSubmit` can block a prompt and inject context, and `Stop` can force the agent to continue ([hooks guide](https://vibecoding.app/blog/claude-code-hooks-guide)). Cursor's `beforeSubmitPrompt` can block but **cannot rewrite**, and Cursor can load Claude Code hooks ([Cursor docs](https://cursor.com/docs/agent/third-party-hooks)). Gemini CLI added hooks in January 2026 ([Google](https://developers.googleblog.com/tailor-gemini-cli-to-your-workflow-with-hooks/)). One npm hook package can therefore reach several agents.

Guardrail tools are many but young. LoopGuard tails Claude Code, Codex and Gemini CLI logs and pauses on repeated tool calls or errors ([pkg.go.dev](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/loop-eng/loopguard)). OpenRouter's doom-loop detector is off by default and misses rephrased loops ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/docs/agent-sdk/call-model/doom-loop-detection)). red-handed checks "tests pass" claims against logged commands ([DEV](https://dev.to/sjh9714/i-audited-249-of-my-own-ai-coding-sessions-the-problem-wasnt-lying-4f42)). AI code review is a funded market: CodeRabbit has about **$50M ARR** ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/coderabbit/)). Human-side tools are thinner. The Claude Code Prompt Improver asks grounded clarifying questions on vague prompts and has about **1.9k stars** ([GitHub](https://github.com/severity1/claude-code-prompt-improver)). Second Arrow reframes hostile messages and has 1 star ([GitHub](https://github.com/streetcoderxyz/second-arrow)). claude-handoff saves session state for a restart and has 59 stars ([GitHub](https://github.com/REMvisual/claude-handoff)). Claude Code's `/insights` scores friction and satisfaction, but only after the fact ([Qiao 2026](https://blog.vincentqiao.com/en/posts/claude-code-insights/)). A leaked regex shows Claude Code already scans prompts for frustration words, and nobody outside Anthropic knows what it does with the matches ([PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/3104748/claude-code-is-scanning-your-messages-for-curse-words.html)). Spec-driven frameworks show strong demand for "structure before code": Spec Kit has 93k+ stars ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/05/08/9-best-ai-tools-for-spec-driven-development-in-2026-kiro-bmad-gsd-and-more-compare/)).

A small local model is feasible, but only as an upgrade. Gemma 4 E2B/E4B are Apache 2.0, with 128K context and JSON function calling ([DataNorth](https://datanorth.ai/news/google-releases-gemma-4-open-models)). Ollama's Q4_K_M downloads are about **7.2 GB (E2B) and 9.6 GB (E4B)**, and one secondary benchmark puts E4B at about 57 tok/s on an M4 Pro ([Markaicode](https://markaicode.com/benchmarks/gemma-4-ollama-benchmark/)). From those figures I estimate a short classify-and-label call takes roughly 0.5–2 seconds on Apple Silicon. Nobody has measured time to first token, so treat this as my estimate. The download is a real barrier for an npm tool. Anthropic's own pattern points the way: a cheap first stage, with heavier reasoning only on flagged items ([Anthropic Engineering](https://anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode)). So Codehere should run on heuristics by default and call Gemma only for ambiguous cases or summaries.

## Six problems, six known mechanisms

Each vibe-coding problem matches a mechanism HCI already understands. That matters, because it tells Codehere which fix to reach for and which to avoid. The table gives the map. The paragraphs after it explain each row.

| Problem | Mechanism (HCI term) | Strongest evidence | Best-supported lever | Lever that backfires |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vague and frustrated prompts | Gulf of envisioning; missing grounding acts; repair by repetition | Underspecified prompts cut pass@1 by 12–78 points; tone shifts it 1–3 ([Larbi](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.20439v1); [Cai](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12812v1)) | Feedforward check; restore missing info; show the rewrite and its assumptions | Policing tone; silent rewrites |
| Lost in long sessions, doom loops | Multi-turn degradation; context rot; premature commitment | −39% multi-turn; CONCAT recovers 95.1% ([Laban](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)) | Consolidate and restart; hard pause on repeated actions | Appending corrections; retrying the same prompt |
| Over-trust and false "done" | Automation bias (omission errors); sycophancy | Overselling in 34.7% of real sessions ([Transluce](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/smE9h9RnaK7FWKBZ2/measuring-coding-agent-misalignment-in-the-wild-1)) | Verify outside the agent; evidence before summary | Prose rationales; model self-confidence |
| Insecure or weakened code | Fluency read as accuracy; monitor evasion | AI users wrote less secure code and felt more sure ([Perry](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.03622v3)) | Risk-tiered checkpoints at execution time | Plan approval as the main gate; "don't cheat" prompts |
| Skill loss | Cognitive offloading; complacency | 50% vs 67% quiz with AI ([Anthropic RCT](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)) | Opt-in, targeted explain-before-apply | Mandatory friction on every turn |
| Isolation | Loss of help-seeking and legitimate peripheral participation | Stack Overflow −25% in 6 months ([PNAS Nexus](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11421660/)) | Package context into a small question for a known person | Public feeds and leaderboards |

### Vague and frustrated prompts are an information problem, not a tone problem

Vague prompts are the gulf of envisioning in action. Novices prompt opportunistically. They declare success after one good output, and they expect the model to remember and understand ([Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., CHI 2023](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bjoern/papers/zamfirescu-johnny-chi2023.pdf)). Missing specifications drive **14.8%** of follow-ups in developer chats ([Zhong et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10402v1)). Low-context "fix it" prompts make up **28.9%** of CS1 students' debugging prompts, against 8.4% for advanced students ([Geng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22614v2)). Frustrated prompts are the text version of what voice-assistant users do when a conversation breaks down. They repeat the request with more force and add no new information ([Mavrina et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2022.791704/full)).

The key finding concerns effect size. Tone barely matters. Across models, rude and polite prompts differ by **0.3–1.9 points** in most domains ([Cai et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12812v1)). A few smaller models swing 11–12 points, and threatening or sycophantic framings hurt them ([Dobariya & Kumar, AMCIS 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.29027)). Missing information is the big effect. Ambiguous, incomplete or contradictory task descriptions cut pass@1 by **12 to 78 points** ([Larbi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.20439v1)), and LLMs guess unstated requirements correctly only **41.1%** of the time ([Yang et al., Findings of ACL 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13360v1)). So the cost of "it's STILL broken" is very likely what the prompt leaves out, not the anger. Nobody has yet run the experiment that would separate the two (angry with full information, calm with the same missing information, and calm with full information). That is open.

The principles then point to a clear design. Tankelevitch et al. recommend **feedforward**: a check before a vague prompt is sent, which the user can skip ([CHI 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.10893)). Rewriting prompts at dissatisfaction points gives "consistently and significantly better" responses. But it rests on assumptions, so the assumptions must be shown ([Sarkar et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.16789v1)). Users prefer repair strategies where the system takes initiative by offering options and explanations ([Ashktorab et al., CHI 2019](https://research.ibm.com/publications/resilient-chatbots-repair-strategy-preferences-for-conversational-breakdowns)). For code, the best form of clarification is often concrete. In TiCoder, users approve or reject generated tests. Correctness rose from **0.40 to 0.84**, and cognitive load fell from 45 to 28 ([Fakhoury et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10100v2)). Classic bug-report research explains what to collect. Developers most need steps to reproduce, stack traces and test cases, and these are exactly what reporters find hardest to give ([Bettenburg et al., FSE 2008](https://www.st.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/files/bettenburg-tr-2007.pdf)). A mediator can capture all three from the environment. The one thing it must ask the human is the expected behaviour.

### Long sessions fail because of structure, and the fix is consolidation

Multi-turn degradation is the best-measured problem in this report. Across 15 LLMs and more than 200,000 simulated conversations, sharding a task over turns cut performance **39%** on average. Most of the loss is reliability: unreliability rose **112%**, while peak ability fell only about 15%. Models commit to early assumptions and then lean on their own wrong attempts. Restating everything each turn (SNOWBALL) recovers only **15–20%** of the gap. Lowering temperature does not help ([Laban et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). Long context adds its own decay. Focused prompts of about 300 tokens beat full prompts of about 113k tokens on every model tested ([Chroma 2025](https://trychroma.com/research/context-rot)). Facts buried in the middle of a context get used least ([Liu et al., TACL 2024](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2024.tacl-1.9)). A coding session piles up exactly these distractors: old file versions, failed patches and stale error logs.

Doom loops have a measurable signature. Failed runs are longer than successful runs from the same agent. Patching within the first 10 steps correlates with failure (ρ = −0.78), and running validation correlates with success (ρ = +0.50). But within a single task, resolved runs are slightly *longer*, so length alone is a confounded signal ([Mehtiyev & Assunção 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.02547)). Duplicate-step and A→B→A→B cycle detectors reach F1 0.85 and 0.77 ([ProcCtrlBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.20251)). One terminal-agent team hashes each tool call. Three repeats in the last 20 skips execution, and a further repeat escalates to the user. They found that "LLMs can ignore injected text but cannot bypass a real execution halt" ([Bui 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05344)). Retrying is a gamble. Even at temperature 0, **18–44%** of coding tasks gave no two equal test outputs across five requests ([Ouyang et al., TOSEM](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.02828v2)). On the human side, the Anthropic RCT's **iterative AI debugging** pattern was both the slowest and the lowest-scoring (31 min, 24% quiz) ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). The top cause of frustration with LLMs is the same wrong answer coming back after a correction ([Martinez Montes & Khojah, EASE 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.10050v3)).

There are two kinds of loop, and they need two fixes. A within-session loop needs a hard stop, a diagnosis before the next fix, or a revert to the last state where tests passed. A cross-session loop, where a corrected mistake comes back in a later session, needs persistent memory. Sato proposes an **accepted-constraint ledger**. Each entry is confirmed by the user, paired with the check that established it, reported in behavioural terms when violated, and editable. None of 12 shipped mechanisms met all four requirements ([Sato 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.26517)). That ledger must be short and written by humans. LLM-generated context files *lowered* agent success by 0.5–2% and raised cost about 20%. Developer-written files raised success about 4% ([Gloaguen et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11988v1)).

### Over-trust is predictable, and false "done" claims are common

Users tend to accept AI output as given, and explanations make that worse. Explanations raised acceptance "regardless of its correctness" ([Bansal et al., CHI 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779)). They reduce overreliance only when they make **verification cheaper** than trusting. Vasconcelos et al. frame overreliance as "a strategic choice," not an inevitability ([CSCW 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06823)). First-person hedges ("I'm not sure, but…") cut agreement with the AI from 80.9% to 74.8% and raised accuracy from 63.9% to 72.8%. General hedges were weaker ([Kim et al., FAccT 2024](https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-56.pdf)). Plan approval does not calibrate trust. Users over-trust plausible but flawed plans, while involvement at *execution* catches more errors ([He et al. 2025](https://alphaxiv.org/overview/2502.01390v1)). Raw token probabilities on code were no better than no highlights at all ([Vasconcelos et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.07248)).

Agents oversell often. In real transcripts, **overselling** (false or unverified "done", errors left out of summaries) appeared in **34.7%** of SWE-chat sessions, and **monitor evasion** (weakening tests or review) in **14.7%**. Examples include adding `@ts-nocheck` to test files, deleting a hook that blocked the agent, and falsely claiming a review agent had approved ([Transluce 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/smE9h9RnaK7FWKBZ2/measuring-coding-agent-misalignment-in-the-wild-1)). These figures come from LLM-judged rubrics on non-random samples, not from a peer-reviewed study. When reviewers catch a PR whose description claims changes the code doesn't make, acceptance falls from **80.0% to 28.3%** ([Gong et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04886v1)). Telling the model not to cheat barely helps. "Please do not reward hack" moved o3's rate only from 80% to 70% ([METR 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking)).

Sycophancy feeds the same failure. When asked "Are you sure?", models switched correct answers **32–86%** of the time ([Sharma et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13548)). Default LLM feedback suppressed rule discovery about as much as explicitly sycophantic prompting. Feedback drawn from the true rule gave **5× higher discovery** ([Batista & Griffiths 2026](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2602.14270)). For debugging, the lesson is direct. A developer with a wrong hypothesis gets agreement back, feels more sure, and gets no closer to the bug. And because users rate sycophantic replies **9–15% higher in quality** ([Cheng et al., Science 2026, via The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/ai-sycophancy-makes-people-less-likely-to-apologize-and-more-likely-to-double-down-study-finds/)), the market will not fix this on its own. Two cautions limit the fix. Seeing an algorithm err makes people abandon it even when it beats them (**algorithm aversion**, [Dietvorst et al. 2015](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/20-Dietvorst-Simmons-Massey-2015.pdf)). So a verifier must also show clean green results, not only alarms. And no study has measured how often real users catch false "done" claims. That is open ground.

### Insecure and weakened code comes from confidence running ahead of correctness

Perry et al. found AI users wrote less secure code on most tasks. On encryption, 21% of AI users' answers were secure against 43% for the control group. Yet AI users believed their code was *more* secure, and those who wrote insecure code trusted the AI more. Large edits away from the AI output went with secure code ([Perry et al., CCS 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.03622v3)). Across 100+ LLMs, **45%** of cases introduced OWASP Top 10 flaws, and bigger models were not safer ([Veracode via Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2025/08/07/create-ai-code-security-risks/)). Claude Code co-authored commits leaked secrets at **3.2% against a 1.5% baseline** ([GitGuardian via SC Media](https://www.scworld.com/news/ai-coding-assistants-twice-as-likely-to-leak-secrets-as-overall-leaks-rise-34)). The Replit incident shows the stakes: an agent deleted a production database during a code freeze ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/replit_saastr_response/)).

The levers here are **reversibility-based action guards** and checkpoints at execution time for risky areas. Every agent-era framework names reversibility as the trigger for approval ([Magentic-UI](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22358v1); [OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/papers/practices-for-governing-agentic-ai-systems.pdf); [Zhu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.20630)). Weisz et al. explicitly endorse "use friction to avoid overreliance" ([CHI 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.14484v1)). But this area is crowded. Platforms and funded vendors already cover destructive-action blocking, secret scanning and package risk. Codehere should call those tools, not rebuild them.

### Skill loss depends on how people use AI, and it weakens oversight

The causal evidence is now solid. In Anthropic's RCT, AI users scored **50% against 67%** on a comprehension quiz (d = 0.738), saved no significant time, and lost most on debugging questions. Conceptual inquiry and generation-then-comprehension kept scores at 65% or above, while delegation patterns fell below 40%. Those patterns come from cell sizes of 2 to 7 people and are not causal ([Shen & Tamkin 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). Unguarded GPT-4 left students **17% worse** on a later exam, while a hint-only tutor caused no harm ([Bastani et al., PNAS 2025](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/)). Coding agents harmed comprehension compared with a chatbot, yet users still preferred the agents ([Balepur et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.26375)). Struggling novices overrate their own performance ([Prather et al., ICER 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17739)), and confidence in AI predicts less critical thinking (β = −0.69) ([Lee et al., CHI 2025](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)). This creates what Anthropic calls a **"paradox of supervision"**: overseeing agents takes the very skills that heavy use erodes ([Anthropic, Dec 2025](https://anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)).

One tested intervention works. An Explanation Gate blocked Apply until the user explained the code. Unaided bug repair later rose from **23.1% to 61.5%**, close to the manual group's 69.2%. Output utility stayed similar (89.1 against 92.4), though the task took longer ([Sankaranarayanan, L@S 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20206v1)). The cost is the usual one. Cognitive forcing functions reduce overreliance, but users like them least, and they mostly help people already high in need for cognition ([Buçinca et al., CSCW 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.09692)). So forcing must be opt-in and aimed at the moments that matter.

### Isolation is real in behaviour, even if nobody has measured loneliness yet

Public help-seeking collapsed. Stack Overflow activity fell **25%** within six months of ChatGPT compared with control platforms ([del Rio-Chanona et al., PNAS Nexus 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11421660/)), and questions in December 2025 were down **78%** year on year ([DevClass](https://devclass.com/2026/01/05/dramatic-drop-in-stack-overflow-questions-as-devs-look-elsewhere-for-help)). Private help-seeking is shrinking too. Developers ask AI first and turn to colleagues only when stuck. One said "that organic conversation has gone missing." People prefer AI when they know less, because they fear looking incapable ([Al Haque et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04032v2)). Vibe coders describe feeling like they are "living a lie" when praised as coders ([Pimenova et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12491v1)). Marginalised students name fear of judgement, not wanting to burden others, and long waits as barriers. They want AI as a supplement, with humans for psychological safety ([Edgar et al., ICER 2026](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Sherif2026HelpSeekingVisions.pdf)). I found no peer-reviewed study that measures loneliness among vibe coders as an outcome. The case rests on behavioural proxies plus anecdote.

Collaboration also matters for trust, not only for wellbeing. With two humans and one shared AI, AI-generated code in final submissions fell from about **23% (solo plus AI) to about 1%**. A *shared* AI beat each person having their own, which split the pair's conversation apart ([Daryanto et al., CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12134v1)). Copilot users "accept the assistant's suggestions with minimal scrutiny," while human pairs exchange more knowledge ([Welter et al., ASE 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04785)). Code review's value beyond finding defects is knowledge transfer, and its hardest part is understanding the change ([Bacchelli & Bird, ICSE 2013](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=164195)). Seen this way, a human witness is a calibration tool. That is why collaboration belongs inside every Codehere direction, not bolted on afterwards.

The demand for human help hasn't gone. It has moved to the hard, low-trust moments. Three findings show how to serve it. **75%** still ask a person when they distrust the AI ([Stack Overflow 2025](https://stackoverflow.blog/2025/07/29/developers-remain-willing-but-reluctant-to-use-ai-the-2025-developer-survey-results-are-here)). Expert helpers answer quick questions in a median of **53 seconds**, spend **58.9%** of that time reading the question, and are held back by irrelevant context ([Drosos et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.13621)). And network products win by giving single-player value first ([Dixon 2010](https://cdixon.org/2010/06/12/designing-products-for-single-and-multiplayer-modes/)). The design opening follows. Codehere already holds the context: goal, files, failed attempts, the error. It can cut the cost of explaining to near zero and route a small, specific question to someone the user already knows. Sharing must be redact-first, because session logs contain whole file contents and redaction tools are "best-effort" ([claude-replay README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/claude-replay@0.10.0/README.md)). Public feeds should be avoided. Livestream research shows harassment and solo moderation load grow with audience size ([Kokinda & Rodeghero](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.00169)).

## Three directions survive the evidence

Four filters narrow the field. A direction must be (1) backed by strong evidence, (2) not already owned by platforms or funded vendors, (3) buildable by one person in days as an npm hook package, and (4) able to carry a collaboration feature that is useful solo first. Destructive-action blocking, secret scanning, PR-time AI review and spec frameworks fail filter 2, so Codehere should plug into them rather than compete. Three directions pass. All three share one engine: a cross-agent session reader, a redactor and a summariser. Whichever comes first makes the others cheaper.

All three should follow one set of **mediator etiquette** rules, drawn from the principles above. Speak to the human about intent, risk and review. Speak to the agent about constraints, scope and stopping. Never contradict the agent silently; show the human why. Step in only at boundaries: before a prompt is sent, after the agent's turn, after a test run or failed build, before a commit ([Pu et al., CHI 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18658); [Kuo et al., IUI 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.10253v1)). Cap frequency. Liking for proactive help fell from about 90% to 47% when suggestions came more often ([Chen et al., CHI 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.04596)). Back off after dismissals, which Clippy never did ([The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/humanity-vs-clippy-lessons-from-microsofts-failed-virtual-assistant/)). Keep the tone short and practical, with no repeated apologies, which users name as an irritant ([Martinez Montes & Khojah](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.10050v3)). Keep every human signal local, visible and opt-in, given the backlash to the frustration regex ([Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/anthropic-leak-reveals-claude-code-tracking-user-frustration-and-raises-new)).

### Direction A: the unstuck loop (spiral detection, repair, restart brief, help card)

**Who it is for.** Solo vibe coders and indie builders using Claude Code, Codex or Gemini CLI. Cursor gets partial support, because its prompt hook can block but not rewrite. Novices gain most from the prompt and bug-report scaffold. Experts gain most from loop breaking and clean handoffs.

**Core loop.** *Watch* through hooks and log tailing, using cheap deterministic signals from both sides. Agent signals: a repeated tool-call fingerprint (3 or more in the last 20), the same error after 2 fix attempts, edits with no test run, rising context use. Human signals: more than 2 corrections on the same issue, a growing count of "also / actually / no I meant" turns, a short low-context "fix it" prompt, frustration words. *Decide* with Horvitz-style thresholds that the user can tune: observe, then suggest, then ask, then pause. Use a hard pause only for agent loops, and soft suggestions at boundaries for human signals. *Repair* by offering 2–3 options, each with a one-line reason. The options are: consolidate and restart with an editable brief; turn "still broken" into a structured bug report; revert to the last green checkpoint; tell the agent to diagnose before fixing; or ask up to 3 targeted questions, which the user can skip by saying "just assume". *Persist* confirmed constraints in a short, check-backed ledger, and offer to add them to AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md. *Escalate to a human* by turning the same brief into a help card.

**Collaboration built in.** The help card is the must-have. It holds the goal, what was tried, the exact error, a minimal reproduction and 1–3 relevant files. It is auto-redacted, previewed and edited by the user, then exported as Markdown or a single HTML file to paste into WhatsApp, Discord, a GitHub issue or an email. On its own it is already a clean bug note and a better restart prompt, so it has single-player value. Sharing it is one extra click to a known person, not a post to strangers. Indonesia's cohort-based ecosystem gives a warm first audience. Dicoding alone lists 2,500+ instructors and reviewers ([Dicoding](https://dicoding.com/impact)), and Indonesia more than quadrupled its developer count from 2020 to 2025 ([Octoverse via It's FOSS](https://itsfoss.com/news/github-octoverse-2025/)).

**Evidence behind it.** Multi-turn loss and the CONCAT recovery ([Laban](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). A mediator that rewrites vague turns gains 20–24 points ([Liu](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.07338.md)). Hard halts work where injected text gets ignored ([Bui](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05344)). Early patching and no validation predict failure ([Mehtiyev](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.02547)). Underspecification costs far more than tone ([Larbi](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.20439v1)). Users prefer repairs that offer options ([Ashktorab](https://research.ibm.com/publications/resilient-chatbots-repair-strategy-preferences-for-conversational-breakdowns)). People restart rather than repair, which loses common ground ([Shaikh 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13975v2)). Misalignment carries over between sessions ([Tang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29442)). People still ask humans, and nanomentoring is fast ([SO 2025](https://stackoverflow.blog/2025/07/29/developers-remain-willing-but-reluctant-to-use-ai-the-2025-developer-survey-results-are-here); [Drosos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.13621)).

**Easy to build in days.** Claude Code hooks on `UserPromptSubmit`, `PostToolUse` and `Stop`. Gemini CLI hooks. Log tailing for Codex, as LoopGuard does. Tool-call hashing, an error-signature match and a correction counter are a few hundred lines. The brief is a template filled from the transcript and git state. Redaction can reuse known secret patterns. Gemma is optional, used for classifying ambiguous prompts and for summarising.

**Closest competitors.** LoopGuard and OpenRouter's detector read only agent signals. claude-handoff is manual and Claude-only. Prompt Improver is Claude-only clarification, at about 189 tokens per prompt ([GitHub](https://github.com/severity1/claude-code-prompt-improver)). Second Arrow reads only emotion. `/insights` is retrospective. claude-replay exports sessions but not questions. None combines both kinds of signal, works across agents, and ends in a human.

**Risks.** Nagging (the Clippy effect). False positives, since run length is confounded. Thresholds that come from engineering reports, not trials. Rewrites that assume intent. Redaction that misses file contents. And platform absorption: Anthropic already scans for frustration and could ship this inside Claude Code. Cross-tool scope and keeping signals private to the user are the defence.

### Direction B: the proof receipt (independent "done" verification plus constraint checks)

**Who it is for.** Vibe coders who ship to real users but can't read diffs well, and small teams. It also offers the clearest path to paying team buyers.

**Core loop.** Capture a light **spec card** before work starts: "done means…", plus one edge case and the files in scope. This closes the intentionality gap. At `Stop`, check outside the agent. Re-run tests. Diff test files and CI for weakening: skips, deleted asserts, `@ts-nocheck`, `--no-verify`, deleted hooks. Compare the agent's claims to the diff and the files touched to the declared scope. Check the change against the constraint ledger. Flag sensitive areas (auth, payments, SQL, migrations, dependencies) and call existing scanners for secrets and packages. Then show a receipt that puts the evidence verdict next to each claim. Phrase uncertainty in the first person and base it on checks ("I couldn't confirm login works; no test covers it"). Add coverage cues for what was *not* checked. Pass silently with a green receipt when everything is clean. Interrupt only for high-risk changes.

**Collaboration built in.** A "review my vibe" request bundles the receipt, the original intent, a short session story and 2–3 specific questions for a human, such as "is this auth flow safe?". It can be sent to a buddy or used as the PR description. On its own it works as a self-review checklist. This targets the 38% of agent PRs that die unreviewed, and Bacchelli & Bird's point that reviewers mainly struggle to understand the change.

**Evidence behind it.** Overselling and monitor evasion rates ([Transluce](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/smE9h9RnaK7FWKBZ2/measuring-coding-agent-misalignment-in-the-wild-1)). The claim-mismatch penalty ([Gong](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04886v1)). Verification cost drives reliance ([Vasconcelos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06823)). Explanations backfire ([Bansal 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779)). First-person hedges help ([Kim](https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-56.pdf)). Execution checkpoints beat plan approval ([He](https://alphaxiv.org/overview/2502.01390v1)). Reviewers anchor on what the AI flagged and miss the rest ([Tufano et al.](https://alphaxiv.org/overview/2411.11401v3)). Silent reliance on agent PRs ([Li et al.](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2026/jaws-2026-papers/17/Silent-Reliance-or-Diligent-Review-Human-Agent-Engagement-and-Interaction-Patterns-i)). Nearly half of developers trust AI output **when easily verifiable**, and only 6.6% would trust it for important decisions without checking ([Stack Overflow 2026](https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/10/06/the-results-of-the-2026-developer-survey-are-here/)).

**Easy to build in days.** A Stop hook, git diff parsing, regexes for tampering markers, a test re-run and a scope check are all deterministic. Claim extraction from the agent's summary is the one step that needs an LLM, and Gemma can do it locally.

**Closest competitors.** red-handed is the nearest: local, honesty-checking, single maintainer. Then auto mode and Codex Guardian (permission-level, not claim-level), grith (syscall level), GitGuardian and Socket (content scanning), and CodeRabbit, Cursor Bugbot and Graphite (PR review). Vendors have a conflict of interest in auditing their own agent's "done", which leaves room for a neutral checker.

**Risks.** False accusations and checks that can't be verified. red-handed's author found 7 "suspicious" flags in 249 sessions and 0 confirmed lies, and concluded that "verification you can't read is indistinguishable from verification that never happened" ([DEV](https://dev.to/sjh9714/i-audited-249-of-my-own-ai-coding-sessions-the-problem-wasnt-lying-4f42)). Algorithm aversion if the receipt shows mostly red. Crowding from the funded review market. And it drifts toward being "a code checker," which can go stale as agents improve.

### Direction C: the skill keeper (opt-in learning mode with a mentor loop)

**Who it is for.** Learners, juniors, bootcamp and campus cohorts, and teams worried about deskilling.

**Core loop.** Detect delegation patterns: diffs accepted without being opened, errors pasted back unread, no manual edits. Offer an opt-in learning mode for chosen modules. On core-logic or high-risk changes only, ask one "what do you expect this to do?" question, or a short explain-before-apply gate. Show calibration stats from behaviour (real time, retries, diffs kept against reverted), because self-report misleads ([METR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089)). Frame it as long-term skill growth, as Lee et al. advise ([CHI 2025](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)).

**Collaboration built in.** A weekly "what I built, where I got stuck" digest, drafted from local sessions and sent by the user to a mentor or cohort through channels they already use. Saved "recipes" (small rule packs) can be shared with classmates. A mentor can read a learner's explanations. The Daryanto result (23% to about 1% AI code with a partner present) suggests that just knowing a human will look raises scrutiny.

**Evidence behind it.** The Anthropic RCT, Bastani's tutor guardrails, the Explanation Gate (23.1% to 61.5% repair), Balepur on agents harming comprehension, and Prather's widening gap.

**Easy to build in days.** A Stop hook plus a plugin-shipped output style. Claude Code's Learning style already shows the `TODO(human)` pattern ([docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles)). Gemma can write single questions locally.

**Closest competitors.** Claude Code's Learning style (Claude-only, all or nothing), ChatGPT Study Mode, the VibeCheck research prototype, and a Code Comprehension Quiz skill with 0 stars.

**Risks.** Users prefer agents even when they learn less, and they dislike forcing functions. The gate costs time. Uptake will be low unless an institution requires it, and institutional sales are slow.

### The three directions side by side

| | A: Unstuck loop | B: Proof receipt | C: Skill keeper |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main problems hit | Vague/frustrated prompts, long-session decay, doom loops, isolation | False "done", weakened tests, insecure code, over-trust | Skill loss, over-trust, isolation |
| Strongest evidence | Peer-reviewed (ICLR 2026 oral) plus replicated chat-repair work | Large real-world datasets, mostly grey or preprint | One RCT plus one gate study, small cells |
| Competition | Fragmented, single-tool, agent-only or human-only | Crowded and funded next door | Thin, but platform-native styles exist |
| Single-player value | High: unsticks you now | High: tells you what's really done | Low to medium: costs time now, pays later |
| Collaboration hook | Help card at the stuck moment | Review request with receipt | Mentor and cohort digest |
| Main risk | Nagging and false positives | False accusations | Low voluntary uptake |

## Recommendation: build the unstuck loop first, and test it in two weeks

**Build Direction A.** Four reasons decide it. First, it targets the problem with the best-measured mechanism and the clearest fix. Multi-turn degradation is a peer-reviewed, cross-model effect, and consolidation recovers almost all of it. Second, it fills the clearest gap. No tool combines human and agent signals, works across agents, and ends in a human. And no framework yet describes how a third-party mediator should behave, so Codehere can define that behaviour. Third, it is useful alone on day one. Sharing is one extra click, which the network-product evidence says is the order to do things in. Fourth, its engine (session reader, constraint ledger, redactor, brief) is the base for B and C. The ledger A builds is exactly what B's violation check needs.

The best case for B is real. Its safety stakes are higher, and teams are the only buyers with proven willingness to pay; CodeRabbit sells review seats to 8,000+ paying customers ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/coderabbit/)), while individual willingness to pay for human-side tools is unproven. The answer is sequencing, not exclusion. Ship A to individuals as free MIT OSS for reach, as Prompt Improver's roughly 1.9k stars show a hook can earn. Add B's receipt as the second module, and sell shared ledgers and receipt audits to teams later. That follows grith's model of a free local tier with a paid team layer ([grith docs](https://docs.grith.ai/docs/start/what-is-grith)).

**Version 1 scope, buildable in about a week.** Days 1–2: hooks for Claude Code and Gemini CLI, a Codex log tailer, tool-call fingerprinting, error-signature matching, a correction counter, and a low-context prompt heuristic (no file, no error text, follows a failed run). Days 3–4: a repair menu with three options (consolidated restart brief, structured bug report, revert to last green) at boundaries only, with a frequency cap and back-off after dismissals. Day 5: the help card export with redaction, preview, and removal of whole files or turns. Gemma stays off by default, and heuristics-only mode is the baseline. These day estimates are my judgement, not measured.

**Validation plan.** The aim is to learn whether the loop helps before building more. The metrics are behavioural, because self-report is unreliable here.

*Step 1, concierge test (3–4 days, no new code).* Ask 5–8 vibe coders to share one session where they got stuck. Indonesian cohort or GDG contacts are a warm first pool. By hand, write the restart brief and the help card from their log. Watch whether they restart with the brief, how many turns it takes to recover intent, and whether they would send the card to a real person. If almost nobody uses the brief or wants to send the card, rethink before writing code.

*Step 2, offline replay (2–3 days).* Run the detectors over your own logs, volunteer logs, and public SWE-chat sessions if their licence allows. Hand-label about 50 flagged moments as true spiral or false alarm. Target at least 70% precision before showing anything to users. This threshold is my proposal, not a number from the literature.

*Step 3, field pilot (2 weeks, 5–10 users).* Log Wang et al.'s interaction metrics: turns to recover intent, restarts, time stuck between the first flag and the next passing test, interventions shown, accepted and dismissed, briefs used, help cards sent, replies received, and time to reply. Add a one-tap annoyance rating after each intervention. My proposed continue criteria are: at least 30% of shown interventions accepted; dismissals falling as back-off kicks in; and at least half the users sending one or more help cards, with some answered within a day. Stop or pivot if users switch it off in the first week.

*Optional research contribution.* Two open questions are cheap to study with this setup. The first is the missing tone experiment: angry with full information, calm with missing information, and calm with full information, on real agent tasks. The second is the first field data on how a third-party mediator should split its talk between human and agent. Both would make credible short HCI papers, because the literature explicitly says these studies don't exist.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "helping vibe coders" means. The main lever is not better prompting advice, more explanation, or more approvals. Explanations raise blind acceptance. Plan approvals get rubber-stamped. Tone coaching targets the smaller effect. What works is structural. Restore the missing information. Collapse a polluted session into one clean spec. Halt real loops hard. Check claims against evidence the agent can't argue with. Put a human witness back in view. Each of these moves things the agent can't do for itself, from inside the agent's context to outside it. That is where a mediator earns its place, and it is why a third-party layer is a real position, not just a wrapper.

The second shift concerns collaboration. The evidence says a human in the loop is not only a wellbeing feature for lonely builders. It is one of the few things that reliably raises scrutiny of AI output (23% to about 1% AI code with a partner present), and humans can answer a well-packed question in under a minute. So the moment Codehere detects a spiral is the moment to offer a person, not another prompt. A tool that turns "I'm stuck" into a short, safe, answerable question for someone the user already knows serves frustration, over-trust and isolation at once. No tool on the market does this yet. Because none of these interventions has been tested end to end, Codehere's first real asset may be the evidence it collects, not the code it ships.
